Scenario 1 IDI · Executive · Multi-cloud

One health standard across forty teams

Global retailer · 40 product teams · 60 clusters on EKS, AKS and GKE · central platform team of 9

The situation

Each business unit ran Kubernetes its own way. Some had resource limits everywhere, others none; some used Helm, others raw manifests; RBAC ranged from strict to cluster-admin-for-all. When a regional outage was traced to a cluster without disruption budgets, leadership asked the platform team which other clusters had the same gap. It took three weeks to answer.

Why it hurts

  • No common definition of "healthy" — every team believed theirs was fine.
  • Platform engineering could not prioritise across teams with objective data.
  • Leadership could not tell whether investment in reliability was paying off.

How Netpin handles it

  1. Connect every cloudAWS, Azure and Google Cloud accounts are added once; EKS, AKS and GKE clusters are discovered and connected with short-lived credentials — no kubeconfig distribution.
  2. One score per projectThe Infrastructure Debt Index scores each team's environment on the same scale: infrastructure health, security, compliance and operational excellence.
  3. Where the points goEach team sees which dimension costs them most and the fixes that recover the most points — so improvement work is self-directed.
  4. Forecasts7, 14 and 30-day projections warn when a team is heading towards a risk threshold.
  5. Leadership viewThe Executive Brief turns the same data into grades and decisions — health, security, audit readiness, cost and delivery.

Time to answer "who else has this gap?"Minutes instead of weeks

Scenario 2 Deploy Gate · Drift · Audit

Replacing a rubber-stamp change advisory board

Financial services group · 1,200 deployments a month · weekly CAB · ServiceNow change tickets

The situation

Every production change needed CAB approval. The board met weekly, reviewed hundreds of tickets in an hour and approved almost all of them — it had no way to assess the actual risk of a change. Meanwhile urgent fixes bypassed the process entirely, creating exactly the unreviewed changes auditors look for.

Why it hurts

  • Release velocity was throttled by a meeting, not by risk.
  • Emergency changes outside the process were a recurring audit finding.
  • High-risk changes were approved as easily as trivial ones.

How Netpin handles it

  1. Gate every pipelineGitHub Actions, GitLab CI and Jenkins send each run to Deploy Gate; the decision is posted back as a commit status.
  2. Risk, not paperworkEach change is scored on privileged or root containers, public exposure, removed limits, open drift on the target, IDI, recent incidents, rollback history, failure rate and dependency health — scaled by blast radius and criticality.
  3. Approve, block or routeLow-risk changes are approved automatically; risky ones are blocked in production or routed to manual review with every contributing factor listed.
  4. Audited overridesOverrides require a written justification and are recorded in audit logs — the evidence auditors ask for.
  5. Catch the bypassesDrift Intelligence detects changes made outside any pipeline and attributes them, closing the emergency-change gap.

What changes — and what to measure

% auto-approvedLow-risk changes that no longer wait for a meeting
Manual changes in productionVisible and trending down
Overrides with justification100% — enforced by the product

Scenario 3 Terraform drift · Attribution

Three teams, one VPC, and a security group nobody owns

Logistics enterprise · 300 Terraform workspaces · state in S3 · console access for 40 engineers

The situation

A security group allowing SSH from anywhere was discovered during a penetration test. Terraform said the group was fine. It turned out an engineer had added the rule in the console during an incident eighteen months earlier, two later Terraform runs had been applied with -target and never touched it, and a different team's module also referenced the group.

Why it hurts

  • A critical exposure existed for eighteen months with nobody aware.
  • terraform plan was run rarely and only per workspace, so nobody saw the difference.
  • Remediation was slow because ownership was unclear.

How Netpin handles it

  1. Three-way comparisonNetpin reads the code from Git, the state from S3 and the live cloud, and classifies the rule as an out-of-band change (code = state ≠ cloud).
  2. Severity from contentPublic SSH on a security group is rated critical and counted in security drift and the IDI security dimension.
  3. AttributionCloudTrail shows who added the rule, when and from the console.
  4. ImpactThe relational graph shows every instance and workload behind the group.
  5. Ready-to-run fixterraform apply -target=aws_security_group.… to revert, or the HCL block to adopt; unmanaged resources get a Terraform import block.

What changes — and what to measure

Questions teams like yours ask

Users belong to companies and projects with role-based permissions. Sign-in supports Google, Microsoft, Apple and any OIDC identity provider such as Okta.

No. Every connection is read-only. Netpin generates fixes for you to run or commit; it never changes your infrastructure.
